# Product Vision

## Product statement

Frontier is a desktop space exploration game about piloting a small spacecraft through a living network of worlds, choosing where to land, and building knowledge through direct observation. The player should feel that every planet is a place with its own physical presence, history and unanswered questions.

The first release direction prioritizes a strong exploration fantasy over a large number of systems. A small number of coherent destinations should feel more memorable than a broad map filled with interchangeable content.

## Player promise

The player can:

1. Leave a world and see it as a physical body in space.
2. Fly toward a destination, judge distance and speed, and choose when to land.
3. Arrive at a grounded location that has a readable environment rather than a menu-only transition.
4. Walk through the location, observe points of interest and receive meaningful survey feedback.
5. Return to orbit and continue deciding where to go next.

## Design principles

### Presence before scale

Camera scale, movement, silhouettes, sound and spatial landmarks should make the player feel present in a place before the project increases the number of planets or map size.

### Curiosity drives progress

Progress should come from finding and understanding places. Missions may provide structure, but the player should be encouraged to look around, compare environments and follow signals rather than only follow waypoint arrows.

### Physical continuity

Orbit, landing, surface movement and departure should read as connected states of the same journey. Loading or scene transitions may be used for production reasons, but the player-facing language should preserve the sense of continuity.

### Distinct worlds, shared rules

Each planet needs a distinct environmental identity. The interaction grammar remains stable: approach, inspect, land, explore, survey and depart. This makes new content learnable without making it visually repetitive.

### Replaceable presentation

Prototype geometry and materials are temporary. Data, gameplay states and interaction contracts should not depend on a particular mesh, texture or model source. Art replacement should improve presentation without forcing a redesign of the core loop.

## Experience quality bar

A destination is ready for broader content production when a player can identify where they are, understand what they can do next, move without fighting the camera or collision, find at least one meaningful landmark and return to orbit without losing context.
